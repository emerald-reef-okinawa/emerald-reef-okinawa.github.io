Swim With Sea Turtles in Okinawa: Spots, Seasons & Tips to Boost Your Chances

Published：2026.06.15
日本語繁體中文한국어

Everything you need to swim with sea turtles in Okinawa: the species you'll meet, the best spots, seasons and times, tips to boost your odds, and the etiquette to follow — from a local guide.

The sea turtle species you can meet in Okinawa

Three sea turtle species are commonly seen around Okinawa. The most frequently encountered is the green sea turtle, a herbivore that grazes on seagrass and algae and relaxes around rocky reefs. Next is the hawksbill turtle, with a pointed beak, which favors sponges in coral crevices. Occasionally a large loggerhead appears too. Snorkelers most often meet green turtles, and individuals used to people may keep feeding nearby. There's no guarantee with wild animals, but Okinawa has one of the highest encounter rates in the world.

Where to find sea turtles on Okinawa's main island

Encounter rates are highest along the central-to-west coast of Okinawa's main island. Maeda Cape in Onna Village is home to resident green turtles, with a high chance of meeting them around the rocky reefs — and you can pair it with the Blue Cave. Other spots include the coral reefs around Onna Village and the calm coves of bridge-connected islands like Kouri and Hamahiga. Turtles patrol set areas, so the key is whether your local guide knows where they are right now. We choose the day's best point based on current conditions.

Tips on season and time of day to boost your chances

Turtles are seen year-round, but spring to autumn (April–November) brings the calmest, clearest water and the best viewing — they feed actively from early summer through fall. Go early morning: calm seas, fewer people, and turtles often feeding in the shallows. Afternoons can get windy and choppy. To improve your odds: ① swim slowly and quietly (no splashing), ② never block a turtle's path, ③ follow your guide. Stay calm and a turtle may even approach you.

Etiquette and rules for swimming with sea turtles

Sea turtles are protected wild animals. To protect both them and your memories, always follow this etiquette: ① Don't touch or chase — it stresses them and damages the protective film on their skin. ② Don't block their path — turtles breathe air and must reach the surface. ③ No flash photography. ④ Don't feed them. ⑤ Keep a proper distance (2m or more). These are universal marine-conservation rules. We always give an etiquette briefing before entering for safe observation for both turtles and people.
